Health Care System in Australia

contrasting the reach of private health insurance (PHI) with the public health care system.

% Privately Insured by Geography

LGAs Ranked by % Privately Insured

Most of the LGAs that have a high proportion of privately insured population are LGAs from high socioeconomic backgrounds, close to city centers.

Remote areas have significantly lower proportion of individuals covered under PHI coverage compared to urban centers.

This can create health outcome inequality between those who live in urban areas compared to those who live in regional areas as those living in regional areas may not be able to access services they require.

Trends in Private Health Insurance Coverage

Use the 'Filter by State' to see PHI distribution by each state.
The 'All States' shows the mean coverage of the age group across states.

Age groups between 20-29 are the least insured group, with a sharp rise in insurance from 30, most likely due to the Lifetime Health Cover Loading.

The percentage of Australians without health insurance started rising from 2015, coinciding with growing premium costs and declining affordability. However, due to COVID-19, public perception regarding health insurance changed, resulting in a sharp uptake of health insurance.

Income vs Private Health Insurance

There is strong positive correlation between median household income and PHI. This can be attributed to the Medicare Levy Surcharge, encouraging the uptake of PHI for high income earners.

LGAs with larger populations (>50k) exhibit a higher base rate of insurance coverage than medium or small LGAs. The regression line for large LGAs is consistently positioned above the others.

This population effect suggests that greater access to private hospitals and ancillary services in urban centers increases the value proposition and uptake, with many smaller LGAs failing to meet the 40% target on average.

Services Paid for Using Private Health Insurance

The primary value proposition of PHI for many consumers is ancillary 'extras' cover which are services like dentists and optometrists, and they are often concentrated in urban centers.

Simultaneously, the public system provides a robust per-capita hospital bed safety net in remote areas

This creates a "push-pull" effect: the main benefit of PHI (extras) is less accessible, while the core risk it covers (hospital) is already addressed by a high-ratio public safety net. This combination significantly weakens the incentive for remote populations to purchase PHI

Public Hospital Beds by State and Remoteness

The public system functions as the comprehensive acute/emergency backbone (managing the vast majority of emergency flows), while the private system operates as a high-volume elective services provider.

Private surgical interventions are almost exclusively pre-planned (elective). Public hospitals, however, must manage a dual demand for surgery, sourced from both elective waitlists and acute emergency admissions.

This reinforces the core value proposition gap for rural residents. Since the benefits of PHI are heavily tied to elective procedures, the lack of local access to these services fundamentally reduces the incentive for coverage.
